About this experiment

Last Stack Standing is a Monte Carlo table-stakes game. Everyone starts equal. Dice decide each round. The only thing that can break symmetry is the ability to create chips from nothing.

Rules

  • Each player starts with 10 chips.
  • Every round, every active player rolls a six-sided die. Highest roll wins. Ties are broken at random.
  • The winner takes the current stake from each other active player (or whatever they have left).
  • A player with 0 chips is eliminated. The last stack standing wins the game.
  • Stakes rise on a schedule so games cannot stall: linear (1, 2, 3…) or exponential (1, 2, 4…).

Money printer

When Player 1 would otherwise be eliminated, they can be recapitalized a limited number of times. The bailout is new chips, sized as a percentage of chips still on the table — not a transfer from anyone else. That is the entire advantage: survival plus an expanding chip supply.

What to look for

  • Fair play: win shares should sit near 1/N, with ordinary sampling noise.
  • Printer: Player 1’s win rate should climb with more bailouts and larger injections.
  • Sweep: maps that curve, with a confidence band, plus knock-on effects: inequality, terminal wealth share, and chips created.

Why compare exists

Running fair and printer back-to-back on the same table settings is the cleanest way to see the printer’s lift. The sweep then answers the follow-up: how much privilege does each extra bailout buy?

Limitations

This is a stylized chance model, not a forecast of any real economy. There is no production, credit, or policy reaction function — only a fair lottery plus optional recapitalization. The analogy is to mechanisms that create claims without a matching loss elsewhere: fiat issuance, bailouts, and similar backstops.

Even when every roll is fair, the option to mint resources when you would have lost is enough to dominate the table. That is the result the charts are built to show.
