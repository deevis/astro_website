The Galton Box

A physical proof that many small random events add up to a predictable curve. Also called a bean machine or quincunx.

Sir Francis Galton

Francis Galton (1822–1911), a British polymath and cousin of Charles Darwin, built this device in the 1890s to show how a normal distribution can emerge from chance. He was studying heredity and variation; the machine made the statistics visible.

Central Limit Theorem

Each peg is a nearly fair coin flip: the ball glances left or right. After n rows the landing slot is the number of rightward moves, k. That count follows a binomial distribution, which for large n is indistinguishable from a normal curve.

P(K = k) = C(n, k) · ½ⁿ  →  μ = n/2,   σ = √(n/4)
  • Independent trials. In the ideal model, each collision is a fresh 50/50 choice.
  • Additive path. The final bin is the sum of those small steps.
  • Many samples. One ball is noise. Hundreds of balls recover the curve.

Why physics disagrees with the textbook

A real (or simulated) board is not a sequence of independent coin flips. Momentum, bounce, size, and the walls all bias the next collision. That is the point of the sliders:

  • Ideal CLT keeps restitution modest so balls split cleanly and the histogram tracks the binomial model.
  • Chaotic raises bounce and speed so paths correlate and the curve fattens or skews.
  • Geometry changes how often a ball actually hits a peg versus slipping through a gap.

The overlay in the stats panel is the fair-binomial prediction. When observed mean and σ drift from theory, the board is teaching the difference between a random-walk model and Newtonian collisions.
